# San Diego State University Field Stations Program: Designing Clear Information for High-Stress Situations

## The Context

I worked with the San Diego State University Field Stations Program during a period when my role expanded well beyond web development.

The program operated four field stations, each with its own identity and communication needs.

I helped create a more unified visual presence across those locations, including websites, logos, brochures, letterhead, photography, and other materials. I also brought the program's web presence into **compliance with Section 508 accessibility** requirements.

But one project in particular changed how I thought about information design.

Fire safety.



## Designing for an Emergency

Wildfire is a very real concern. Not just for Southern California in general, but also for the biological field stations.

The Field Stations Program needed a website that could communicate changing fire conditions, threat levels, evacuation information, and downloadable safety materials.

This wasn't a site people would necessarily browse casually. Sometimes, visitors might arrive because something was wrong and they needed to know what to do. 

That changed the design decisions.

**During an emergency, people don't need clever navigation**. They don't need decoration. What they *do* need is to know:

- What's happening?
- How serious is it?
- What am I supposed to do?
- Where do I go?

The interface needed to make those answers obvious. 

The system I created was able to **react on-the-fly to real-time danger levels**. Under low to moderate fire danger levels, a user would get the full website, with planning information, activity booklets for children, and other educational materials. 

But during a fire emergency, **the site automatically switched** over to an extremely low-data version that could be accessed on cell phones, giving just the needed information such as escape routes, evactuation orders, and emergency services. 



## Clarity Before Decoration

The project reinforced something that would influence much of my later UX work: the more stressful the situation, the less cognitive effort an interface should require.

Hierarchy, language, color, and accessibility all matter.

Information needs to be understandable quickly, without requiring users to interpret the design before they can interpret the message.

That lesson would eventually apply in very different environments later in my career, from scientific instruments to design systems, but the underlying principle remains the same.

**Good interface design isn't about making information look interesting. It's about making the next decision easier.**
