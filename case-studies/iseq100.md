# iSeq 100: Making Complex Science Easier to Use

## The Challenge

When I joined Illumina, much of its software had been designed for highly experienced scientists working with sophisticated sequencing equipment.

The iSeq 100 was different.

It was a smaller, more approachable sequencing system intended to bring next-generation sequencing into environments that might not have dedicated sequencing experts.

That changed the design problem. A lab technician operating an iSeq 100 shouldn't need a PhD in genetics to understand what the software was asking them to do.

**My job was to help make an extraordinarily complex scientific process feel understandable**.

---

## Designing the Workflow

I owned the user-facing workflow for the iSeq 100 control software, including the interaction design, interface design, animations, and transitions that guided users through a sequencing run. This required understanding much more than the screen in front of me.

The software had to coordinate with physical hardware, backend processes, consumables, system states, and the expectations of people using the instrument.

**I worked across front-end and backend software teams, hardware engineers, marketing, UX, and other stakeholders to understand those dependencies and translate them into a coherent user experience.**

In many ways, my role was to connect the dots.

---

## Prototyping the Experience

Rather than relying entirely on static mockups, **I built functional prototypes** that allowed us to experience interactions and workflows before they were committed to production. 

This mattered because interaction problems can be difficult to see in a sequence of static screens. A workflow might look perfectly reasonable in a design file and still feel confusing once someone actually tries to move through it.

**Functional prototypes gave us a way to test assumptions**, incorporate findings from user testing, demonstrate animations and transitions, **and identify problems earlier**, when they were still relatively inexpensive to change.

---

## Designing for Someone Like Me

One unusual advantage I brought to the project was that I wasn't a geneticist. I had to learn what the terminology meant and why each step existed.

Rather than treating that as a weakness, I found it useful.

If *I* couldn't understand an instruction, there was a reasonable chance that someone operating the instrument for the first time might struggle with it too.

That made me particularly sensitive to terminology, hierarchy, feedback, and the amount of information we expected users to understand at each step.

**The goal wasn't to remove the science, it was to reveal only as much complexity as the user needed at that moment.**

---

## Seeing It Become Real

**One of the proudest moments of my career was seeing the interface running on the actual production instrument as the iSeq 100 prepared to ship.**

For months, the experience had existed as sketches, prototypes, design files, discussions, animations, and code. Then suddenly it was part of a physical product.

Something we had been working on, debating on, and user testing was **now going into laboratories around the world**.

The interaction and interface work I contributed to the iSeq 100 ultimately resulted in **three U.S. design patents** covering aspects of its interface and workflow:

- US D873281 S
- US D895640 S
- US D932500 S

But the patents aren't what I remember most about the project. I remember the craftsmanship.

The iSeq 100 gave me an opportunity to concentrate deeply on one experience, refining interactions, questioning terminology, testing workflows, and **making something technically complex feel progressively simpler**.

It remains one of my favorite projects because it demonstrated something I've believed throughout my career:

**Complexity isn't necessarily the problem. Confusion is.**
