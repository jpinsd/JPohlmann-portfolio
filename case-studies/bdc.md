# Business.com: Bridging Design and Engineering During the Responsive Web Transition

## Modernizing the Front End

When I joined Business.com, I was the primary front-end and UX specialist on a team made up largely of backend engineers.

My role eventually covered the entire path from interface concept to production code. I created wireframes, worked through interaction and visual design, and then implemented those designs using PHP, HTML, CSS/Sass, JavaScript, and jQuery.

It was a period when the web itself was changing quickly and responsive design was becoming the expectation rather than the exception. CSS tooling was evolving, and websites were moving away from rigid desktop-oriented layouts.

Much of my work involved helping Business.com make that transition.


## Before Sass

The existing front end had accumulated years of CSS, including an outdated CSS framework. Maintaining it was difficult, and seemingly small visual changes could require hunting through large stylesheets to understand what depended on what.

When I started at Business.com, Sass still wasn't robust enough for what we needed, so **I developed a custom solution** using PHP to parse styles, variables, and functions and output CSS. This sustained us for a while, but I kept my eye on the progress of Sass as a tool to use in out build process. 

Once it became a solid choice, I helped move the front end toward Sass and a more structured approach to reusable styles. The reason was for performance as much as for maintainability of styles. 

While PHP and other server-side rendering technologies are still preferable, in my mind, to front-end heavy applications, Sass allowed us to compile the CSS before going to production. On our already heavy pages, every little bit helped to decrease load times and increase overall user experience. 




## Responsive Design

One of the most important changes was moving away from treating desktop, tablet, and mobile as separate designs. I worked closely with the lead designer, **translating designs into responsive interfaces** and making decisions about what should happen in the spaces between the supplied compositions.

Eventually, something interesting happened. The designer stopped creating separate tablet and mobile comps for me.

He told my manager he didn't need to. He would give me the desktop design and trusted me to understand how it should adapt. He jokingly referred to what happened between desktop and mobile as **JP doing his "magic."**

There wasn't any magic involved. I had simply developed a strong understanding of how layouts should flow and how components should respond as available space changed. That trust remains one of my favorite compliments from my career.



## Translating Design

A large part of my job was translating between disciplines. 
- Designers shouldn't have to understand every browser limitation to explore an idea. 
- Backend developers shouldn't have to become interaction designers to implement it. 

**I could work between those worlds**, understanding the design intent, recognizing the technical constraints, and finding an implementation that respected both.

That role would become increasingly important throughout the rest of my career.



## The CSS Guy

Because most of the engineering team specialized in backend development, I naturally became the person people came to when something involved CSS, responsive behavior, or unusual front-end interactions.

I enjoyed that position because it wasn't *just* about fixing styles. It gave me opportunities to explain *why* something behaved the way it did and to **help other developers** become more comfortable working in the browser.



## What I Learned

Business.com was where responsive design stopped feeling like a technique and started becoming a way of thinking. The browser isn't a collection of fixed canvases, it's a continuously changing environment.

Once I understood that, I stopped thinking primarily about how pages should look at particular widths and started thinking about how interfaces should behave everywhere between them.

That idea would follow me into PuppySpot, STUDS, and much of my later work.
