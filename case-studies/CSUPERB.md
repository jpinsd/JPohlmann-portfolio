# CSUPERB: Turning a Manual Academic Workflow into Software

## The Problem

The California State University Program for Education and Research in Biotechnology (CSUPERB) managed grant proposals, conference abstracts, peer review, and related academic workflows across the California State University system.

Much of that work depended on manual administrative processes: submissions had to be collected, reviewers assigned, materials organized, decisions tracked, and conference information eventually had to be assembled into printed materials.

The challenge wasn't simply putting forms online, it was about understanding the entire workflow.



## Building the System

**I was the sole developer on the project**. I designed and built a web-based system that supported three primary roles:
- applicants and submitters
- reviewers
- administrators

The system included authentication, database-driven submissions, administrative tools, reviewer workflows, and the underlying logic needed to move information through the process.

I handled the database, PHP development, HTML, CSS, authentication, and administration interfaces.

Today we'd probably call it a full-stack application. But at the time, I just thought of it as **solving a problem**.



## Automating the Work Around the Work

One of the most useful parts of the system had little to do with the submission form itself. Once the information was structured in a database, we could reuse it. 

Data that previously had to be manually collected and reformatted for conference materials could now be generated automatically. A process that had consumed an enormous amount of administrative time could happen in minutes.

That was the part I found most satisfying. The best automation isn't necessarily the part users see. Sometimes it's **the workload that simply disappears**.



## What I Learned

CSUPERB taught me to look beyond the immediate feature I was being asked to build.

Putting a submission form online solved one problem. But understanding where that information came from, who needed it next, and what ultimately happened to it revealed opportunities to improve the entire process.

That became an important lesson for me: **Don’t just automate the task. Understand the workflow around it**.

Sometimes the greatest improvement isn’t making one step faster. It’s recognizing that an entire step no longer needs to exist.
